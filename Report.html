I scored the political ideology of all 790 users posting climate-pollen tweets using the package tweetscores. The scores were calculated based on the influential (political) account an user is following. Many users cannot be scored as they did not authorize data retrieval, deleted their accounts, or did not follow influential accounts.

As a contrast, I also randomly selected 1000 pollen tweets, obtained a list of unique users, and scored their political ideology.

General belief

Research question 1: Is there a partisan bias on how likely people are to believe in the climate change impacts on pollen season?

Hypothesis 1: A left-leaning user is more likely to express belief and less likely to express disbelief in climate change impacts on pollen season compared to a right-leaning user, if they both talk about pollen. Specifically, the statistical distributions of political ideology scores differ between those who discuss pollen and those who also expressed belief or disbelief in climate change impacts. Here is a comparison of the distributions of political ideology scores in the wide user group discussing pollen (blue) and among the smaller user group (356 users) (red) that stated belief in climate change impacts.

The differences between the two distributions was not statistically significant (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p > 0.05), although the general comparison of the proportion of left vs. right individuals was consistent with our hypothesis.

Knowledge production

Research question 2: Is there a partisan bias on how people communicate this belief personally, without an immediate influence of media?

Hypothesis 2: A left-leaning user is more likely to personally comment on climate change impacts on pollen season compared to a right-leaning user, if they both talk about pollen. Specifically, the statistical distributions of political ideology scores differ between those who discuss pollen and those who also expressed belief in climate change impacts based on personal opinion.

There were 27 users expressed belief on climate change impacts on pollen seasons based on personal opinion (red). The partisan bias on personal opinion was marginally significant (p = 0.057), being a lot stronger than the non-significant bias on belief based on expert opinion and personal opinion taken together.

Message 2: The lack of overall partisan bias shown in Question 1 may be attributed to a top-down approach, i.e., large exposure to expert opinions, communicated through scientific articles, news reports, and experts’ personal communication. On the other hand, the knowledge distribution via the bottom-up approach, i.e., personal opinion by non-experts, is still likely to have a partisan bias. Although the message of climate change impacts on pollen season may reach right-leaning users well, the message may be less likely to be amplified through personal communications in right-leaning communities.

Knowledge distribution

Research question 3: Is there a partisan bias in the distribution of this belief in the social network?

Hypothesis 3: A user is more likely to receive and distribute information on climate change impacts on pollen season from a user or similar ideology. Specifically, the ideology of the target user (user who expressed belief) has a statistically significant correlation with the ideology of the source (mentioned, retweeted, or replied user).

As a preliminary analysis, I explored a network around influential nodes in the network communicating climate change impacts on pollen season.

Message 3: Although both left- and right-leaning users have generally similar belief in the climate change impacts on pollen season, possibly attributed to the exposure to expert opinion, the partisan bias is still very strong in terms of the source of information. People are more likely to receive information from sources with a similar political ideology. This result is consistent with the information theory in social psychology, suggesting that individuals choose to interact with others who hold similar beliefs (homophily). It is encouraging that this knowledge of climate change impacts on pollen season reach diverse audience, but there may still be information silo in the communication of this knowledge across partisans.
